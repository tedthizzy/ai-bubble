# Data-center siting and large-load tariffs, September 16, 2026

The video's warning about opposition to data centers has observable policy consequences. These records establish specific local restrictions. They do not measure national public opinion, national permitted MW, or how many planned AI campuses will be delayed. A moratorium, a proposed ordinance, an approved tariff, and a grid-service application are distinct states.

- [Seattle Ordinance 127447](https://seattle.legistar.com/LegislationDetail.aspx?FullText=1&GUID=86F14A6D-9247-4BB5-839E-0A26937BFD3C&ID=8032163) passed June 9 and was signed June 11. It bars filing, processing, and approval of applications to establish or expand data centers covered by the ordinance while the city studies permanent rules. The [City Council's explanation](https://council.seattle.gov/2026/06/09/city-council-passes-emergency-data-center-moratorium-and-policy-framework/) describes the covered scale as more than 20 MVA and the duration as one year, with a possible six-month extension. This is an enacted rule, not a pending proposal.
- [Lake County, Illinois](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/ILLAKE/bulletins/42810a1) approved an eight-month moratorium on September 8 for unincorporated areas, lasting through May 2027. The county reports more than 1,200 online comments. It also says it had no data-center proposal in those unincorporated areas, so this rule cannot be counted as a delay to a named project on the evidence checked.
- [Gilroy, California](https://www.cityofgilroy.org/m/newsflash/Home/Detail/1331) approved a citywide 45-day urgency moratorium on September 14, effective immediately, with statutory extension options. The city says an already approved Amazon data center remains under construction and is unaffected. A possible second Amazon facility had reached pre-application only. The rule restricts future applications, not the site's existing approved phase.
- [Woodburn, Oregon](https://www.woodburn-or.gov/1133/Data-Center-Moratorium) declared a 120-day moratorium on September 14. The city says it has no pending or active data-center applications. Its official page supplies the adopted resolution and a hearing record.

Grid policy is moving in both directions. [FERC's June 18 order](https://www.ferc.gov/news-events/news/ferc-launches-aggressive-targeted-action-speed-large-load-integration) directed six regional grid operators to justify or reform large-load tariffs and studies, seeking faster connections while preventing cost shifts to other customers. [Missouri's Public Service Commission](https://psc.mo.gov/General/Utility_Tariffs_for_Large_Load_Customers) reports approved large-load tariffs at Ameren Missouri and Evergy: a 12-year minimum service term after an optional ramp, two years of minimum-bill collateral, exit fees, and an 80% minimum demand-charge billing level. Liberty's tariff case remained in progress on the page checked. Those terms move more cancellation and underuse risk to a large-load customer; they are not proof that a particular AI tenant has signed or posted collateral.

The policy implication for this repo is a project-level question: was the site already approved before a local rule, and which tariff governs its actual utility service? None of the four local examples supplies an independently measured national effect on 2027 installed AI compute. The [50-state and DC utility source-system audit](state_utility_source_audit_2026-09-16.md) is an entry point for jurisdiction-specific filings; it is not a complete local ordinance census.

Additional primary-source audit and correction, September 16 Pacific:

- [New York Executive Order 62](https://www.governor.ny.gov/executive-order/no-62-establishing-temporary-moratorium-data-centers-new-york-while-state-develops), signed July 14, directs the Department of Environmental Conservation to hold applications for discretionary state permits related to construction or expansion of data centers that can consume at least 50 MW, until the state's generic environmental impact statement and findings are complete. It applies only to DEC applications that were not deemed complete before the order. The order expressly excludes local-government permits and certain research, education, manufacturing, and medical facilities. The governor describes the process as taking up to a year; the order itself ties the pause to completion of the study. Its nearly 12 GW of New York data-center load requests as of May 2026 are interconnection requests, not operating load or identified delayed projects. The proposed grid fund and other possible payment obligations in the order are directions to study, not approved tariffs.
- The earlier Savannah exclusion was too cautious after checking only its agenda. [Savannah's September 10 action minutes](https://www.savannahga.gov/DocumentCenter/View/37854/ActionMinutes_2026-09-10) record a 9-0 Council vote approving item 14 after first and second readings. The [attached ordinance text](https://agenda.savannahga.gov/content/files/savannah-data-center-moratorium-ordinance-v2_1.docx) describes a 155-day pause on applications for new or expanded data centers of at least 10 MW and on conversion of warehouses or industrial buildings of at least 200,000 square feet to data-center use, including pending applications held in abeyance. It preserves specified prior-permit and reliance claims. Its effectiveness is conditioned on mayoral signature; the action minutes do not record that signature, and no enrolled signed copy was located in the checked city sources. Thus the verified status is Council-approved, with final effectiveness unverified, not merely proposed or conclusively in force. The city sources do not identify a specific project delayed by this vote.
- [Seattle's Council account](https://council.seattle.gov/2026/06/09/city-council-passes-emergency-data-center-moratorium-and-policy-framework/) says four companies had approached Seattle City Light about five facilities with combined maximum demand of 369 MW. This is a concrete set of utility approaches in the jurisdiction of its enacted moratorium, not five permitted sites, 369 MW energized, or a measured amount of canceled supply. [Ordinance 127447](https://seattle.legistar.com/LegislationDetail.aspx?FullText=1&GUID=86F14A6D-9247-4BB5-839E-0A26937BFD3C&ID=8032163) also exempts qualifying expansions of operating facilities by no more than an additional 20 MVA.
- [Virginia's State Corporation Commission](https://www.scc.virginia.gov/about-the-scc/scc-facts/) approved Dominion's separate GS-5 class for customers demanding at least 25 MW, effective January 1, 2027. New contracts from that date require at least 14 years of service. Certain large-load customers owe minimum billed demand of 85% of contracted distribution and transmission capacity and 60% of generation capacity; lower-credit customers may have to secure up to 60% of minimum contract charges. The SCC's August 2026 Rider T1 order approved a prospective framework for direct assignment of dedicated connection-line costs; a separate docket still had to set the line-extension policy details. These are approved customer-risk terms, with the later direct-assignment details still pending, and no particular site's executed contract established by the SCC summary.
- [AEP Ohio's approved Data Center Tariff](https://www.aepohio.com/company/about/rates/data-center-tariff/) took effect July 23, 2025 for qualifying new load. The term is the ramp period, at most four years, plus eight years. AEP describes a minimum monthly billed-demand formula capped at 85% of total contract capacity; that is more precise than treating 85% of contract capacity as every customer's bill in every month. Customers without specified high credit and liquidity must provide a guarantee or collateral equal to 50% of full-term minimum charges. Before energization, a signed letter of agreement makes the customer reimburse 100% of buildout costs if it cancels or delays more than 12 months. AEP is studying another application tranche from October 1, 2026; a load-study request or contract estimate still is not energized demand.
- Faster connection is also an active policy choice. [FERC's June 5 SPP order](https://spp.org/documents/76880/20260605_order%20-%20revisions%20to%20add%20the%20conditional%20high%20impact%20large%20load%20service_er26-1323.pdf) accepted Conditional High Impact Large Load Service effective July 1, subject to compliance conditions. It permits temporary non-firm service for one to seven years while generation and network upgrades for firm service are developed. This can advance connection timing, but the service is subject to curtailment behind firm service and is not evidence of continuously available AI compute.

September 16 public-opinion and federal-legislative observation: The [AP-NORC/University of Chicago EPIC survey](https://climate.uchicago.edu/news/concern-about-ais-environmental-impact-has-risen-over-the-past-year-especially-among-young-adults/) interviewed 3,424 US adults July 6-24 using probability and non-probability panels; its reported overall margin of sampling error is +/-2.2 percentage points. It found 53% extremely or very concerned about AI's environmental impact, up from 41% in 2025; 84% were at least somewhat concerned about local electricity-price effects of data centers. The different thresholds matter: the [EPIC chart summary](https://epic.uchicago.edu/insights/2026-poll-americans-views-on-the-impact-of-ai-and-data-centers/) says 63% were extremely or very concerned about electricity prices. The survey reports 73% support for making data centers cover grid-upgrade costs and 62% support for limiting new construction. These are national attitudes, not a vote forecast or measured project cancellations.

The [House Energy and Commerce Committee reports](https://energycommerce.house.gov/posts/ratepayer-protection-act-passes-house-with-strong-bipartisan-support) that H.R. 9340, the Ratepayer Protection Act, passed the House 417-3 on September 16. The bill would direct state utility commissions to *consider* large-load standards for data centers above 100 MW so those loads bear full incremental service costs. House passage does not make those standards law or establish any developer's actual tariff or new expense. The vote is evidence that ratepayer concerns now have bipartisan federal legislative attention; the enacted effect is still zero at this stage.
